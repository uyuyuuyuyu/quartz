# Notes on the Discharging Method

The discharging method is a graph theory technique primarily used to prove the existence of specific structures within planar graphs.

### 1. Key Concepts and Definitions
* **Triangulations:** Let $G$ be a triangularization; the neighborhood of every vertex with a degree of at least 3 contains a spanning cycle. If we consider the region containing vertices $v_i$ and $v_{i+1}$, since every region is a triangle, $v_i, v_{i+1} \in E(C)$.
* **Configurations:** We will use "Configuration" to mean a subgraph with added stipulations (such as degrees). An example is an edge where both endpoints have degree 5.
* **Initial Charge & Euler's Formula:** For a planar graph $G$ where $|G|=n$ and $||G||=m$, we can define an initial charge $\mu(v) = 6 - d(v)$. Based on Euler's identity, the sum over all vertices is $\sum_{v \in V(G)} \mu(v) = 6n - 2m \ge 12$. Equality holds if $G$ is a triangulation.

### 2. General Architecture of a Discharging Proof
Discharging is a bookkeeping method that helps us use global relations (such as Euler's identity) to prove the existence of certain structures. Charge can be moved around according to rules, but it can be tracked and not created or destroyed.

The standard four steps of a discharging proof are:
1. Set up initial charge (e.g., $\mu(v) = 6 - d(v)$).
2. List discharge rules.
3. Show some vertex must have a particular final charge (e.g., positive).
4. The existence of a vertex with that final charge implies the existence of the desired configuration.

### 3. Wernicke's Theorem (1904) & Associated Lemma
* **Theorem:** If a planar graph has minimum degree 5, then it has an edge $xy$ such that $d(x) = 5$ and $d(y) \le 6$.
* **Lemma:** If a triangularization has minimum degree 5, then there is an edge $xy$ such that $d(x) = 5$ and $d(y) \in \{5, 6\}$.
* **Proof Sketch:** Let $G$ be a triangularization with $\delta(G) = 5$. Assign initial charge $\mu_0(v) = 6 - d(v)$; recall the sum is $\ge 12 > 0$.
* **Discharge Rule:** Every vertex of degree 5 donates $\frac{1}{5}$ charge to each of its neighbors.
* Since the sum of final charges is the same as the sum of initial charges, there exists a vertex $v$ such that $\mu^*(v) > 0$.
* A classic example of a planar graph with $\delta(G) = 5$ is an icosahedron.

### 4. Edge Degree Sums in Triangulations
* **Theorem:** Let $G$ be a triangulated planar graph with $\delta(G) \ge 3$. Then there exists an edge $xy \in E(G)$ such that $d(x) + d(y) \le 13$.
* **Proof Sketch:** Suppose by contradiction that for all $xy \in E(G)$, $deg(x) + deg(y) \ge 14$.
* Assign $\mu(v) = 6 - d(v)$. The sum over all vertices is $\ge 12 > 0$.
* Therefore, there must be some vertex $v \in V(G)$ such that $\mu^*(v) > 0$.
* For vertices with degree $d(v) \in \{3, 4, 5\}$, the final charge is $\mu^*(v) = 0$.
* Since $N(v)$ is spanned by a cycle, and no donor vertices share an edge, at most $\lfloor \frac{deg(v)}{2} \rfloor$ neighbors can donate to $v$.
* If $d(v) \ge 11$, the maximum donation from any neighbor is 1. Thus, the final charge $\mu^*(v) \le (6 - deg(v)) + \lfloor \frac{deg(v)}{2} \rfloor$. This simplifies to $6 - \lceil \frac{deg(v)}{2} \rceil \le 0$.
* Because no vertex can maintain a positive final charge, we reach a contradiction, proving the theorem.
