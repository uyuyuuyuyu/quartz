## Corollary to Theorem 9.2: Bipartite Planar Graphs
* **Theorem**: In any bipartite planar graph G of order n >= 3, the number of edges m is bounded by m <= 2n - 4.
* **Proof Idea**: The proof is very similar to the proof of Theorem 9.2 and uses Euler's Identity, 2 = n - m + r.
* Since G is bipartite, all cycles have an even length, meaning the smallest cycle has a length of 4.
* Assuming every region is bounded by a cycle, the sum of the boundary edges yields 2r <= m.
* Applying Euler's Identity results in m <= 2n - 4.

## Application: Nonplanarity of K_3,3
* For the complete bipartite graph K_3,3, the number of vertices is n = 6 and the number of edges is m = 9.
* Plugging into the bipartite edge bound inequality gives 2(6) - 4 = 8, and 9 is not less than or equal to 8.
* Because this necessary condition fails, K_3,3 is nonplanar.

## Corollary 9.3: Minimum Degree
* **Theorem**: Every planar graph with at least 3 vertices has at least one vertex of degree 5 or less.
* **Proof Idea**: Let G be a connected planar graph with n >= 2 and m <= 3n - 6.
* The sum of all vertex degrees is 2m, which evaluates to <= 6n - 12.
* Suppose by way of contradiction that G has at most 2 vertices of degree <= 5.
* This assumption forces the sum of degrees to be >= 6(n - 2) + 2(0), which creates a contradiction.
* This shows that if G is planar, its minimum degree is <= 5.

## Platonic Solids
* We can visualize polyhedra as planar graphs.
* A Platonic solid is a polyhedron where all faces have the same-length cycle and the graph is regular, meaning the same number of edges meet at each vertex.
* **Claim**: There are exactly 5 Platonic solids.
* **Proof Idea**: Let G be a planar graph that is d-regular, with every face bounded by a cycle of length g.
* Using Euler's Identity, we get 1/d - 1/2 + 1/g = 1/m > 0, which means 1/d + 1/g > 1/2.
* Since d >= 3 and g >= 3, the only valid integer pairs for d and g are in the set {3, 4, 5}.
* These pairs correspond exactly to the Tetrahedron, Cube, Dodecahedron, Octahedron, and Icosahedron.

## Subdivisions and Kuratowski's Theorem Preparation
* Both K_3,3 and K_5 are nonplanar graphs.
* If a graph has K_3,3 or K_5 as a subgraph, it is nonplanar.
* A subdivision of a graph is obtained by replacing some edges with paths, effectively inserting vertices of degree 2.
* Every subdivision of a nonplanar graph remains nonplanar.
* Consequently, if a graph contains a subdivision of K_5 or K_3,3, it is nonplanar.
