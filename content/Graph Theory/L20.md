# Crossing Numbers and Probabilistic Bounds

## Introduction to Crossing Number

**Final project note:** If your paper ends up being too technical, check its discussion/introduction for earlier results.

### $\S$ Crossing Number (Not in book)
The **crossing number** of a graph $G$, denoted $\text{cr}(G)$, is the minimum number of edge crossings over all drawings of $G$.

**Note:** $G$ is planar if and only if $\text{cr}(G) = 0$.

**Examples:**
* $K_5$ is non-planar, so $\text{cr}(K_5) \ge 1$.
    * By construction, $\text{cr}(K_5) \le 1$.
    * Therefore, $\text{cr}(K_5) = 1$.
* $K_{3,3}$ is non-planar, so $\text{cr}(K_{3,3}) \ge 1$.
    * In fact, $\text{cr}(K_{3,3}) = 1$.

*In general, $\text{cr}(G)$ can be hard to compute.*

**Remark:** In a plane drawing of a graph $G$ with $\text{cr}(G)$ crossings, every crossing occurs between nonadjacent edges. (Adjacent edges crossing never happens).

## Lower Bounds on Crossing Number

**Proposition:** Let $G$ be a graph with $\|G\| = m$. Let $k$ be the maximum number of edges in any planar subgraph of $G$. Then:
$$\text{cr}(G) \ge m - k$$

**Proof:**
In a drawing of $G$ with $\text{cr}(G)$ crossings, remove one edge from each crossing. The result is planar. We removed $\le \text{cr}(G)$ edges and were left with $\le k$ edges.
So, $m \le \text{cr}(G) + k \implies \text{cr}(G) \ge m - k$.

**Corollary:** For any graph $G$ with $n$ vertices and $m$ edges:
$$\text{cr}(G) \ge m - (3n - 6)$$

**Observations:**
This bound gives us:
* $\text{cr}(K_5) \ge 10 - (15 - 6) = 1$, and $\text{cr}(K_5) = 1$.
* $\text{cr}(K_6) \ge 15 - (18 - 6) = 3$. (In fact, $\text{cr}(K_6) = 3$).

## The Crossing Lemma

**Theorem (Ajtai-Chvátal-Newborn-Szemerédi 1982, Leighton 1983):**
Let $G$ be a graph, $|G| = n$, $\|G\| = m$. If $m \ge 4n$, then:
$$\text{cr}(G) \ge \frac{m^3}{64n^2}$$

*Aside: The proof is probabilistic, so let's state some probability results.*

## Probability Prerequisites

### Expected Value
The **expectation** of a discrete random variable $X$ with sample space $\mathcal{X}$ is:
$$\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} x \Pr(X = x)$$
and can be thought of as a long-term average.

**Example:** Let $X$ be rolling a fair 6-sided die.
$$\mathbb{E}(X) = 1(1/6) + 2(1/6) + 3(1/6) + 4(1/6) + 5(1/6) + 6(1/6)$$

### Linearity of Expectation
Expectation is **linear**, even when variables are not independent.
Let $X, Y$ be discrete random variables with sample spaces $\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y}$.

$$\mathbb{E}(X + Y) = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} (x + y) \Pr(X = x \text{ and } Y = y)$$
$$= \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} x \Pr(X = x \wedge Y = y) + \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} y \Pr(X = x \wedge Y = y)$$
$$= \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \left[ x \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \Pr(X = x \wedge Y = y) \right] + \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \left[ y \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \Pr(X = x \wedge Y = y) \right]$$
$$= \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} [x \cdot \Pr(X = x)] + \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} y \Pr(Y = y)$$
$$= \mathbb{E}(X) + \mathbb{E}(Y)$$

**Fact:** If $X$ is always greater than $Y$, then $\mathbb{E}(X) \ge \mathbb{E}(Y)$.

### Indicator Variables
Consider a discrete random variable $X$ and an outcome we care about, $x$. We can define an "indicator variable" $F$ as:
$$F = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } X = x \\ 0 & \text{else} \end{cases}$$

**Example:** Imagine 10 coin-flips. 
$$F_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } i^{\text{th}} \text{ flip is H} \\ 0 & \text{else} \end{cases}$$
Then the total number of heads is $\sum_{i=1}^{10} F_i$.

**Observation:** Suppose an experiment is repeated $N$ times. Each instance has an indicator variable $(F_1, \dots, F_N)$. Say the probability of the event measured by the indicator variable is $p$. Then, the expected number of times that event happens is:
$$\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^N F_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}(F_i) = \sum_{i=1}^N [1 \cdot p + 0 \cdot (1 - p)] = Np$$

## Proof of the Crossing Lemma

**Overview:**
Given a graph $G$, randomly choose an induced subgraph $H$ of $G$. The expected number of crossings in $H$ can be computed in terms of $\text{cr}(G)$. Combine with the Corollary $\text{cr}(G) \ge m - (3n - 6)$ to get the desired bound on $\text{cr}(G)$.

**Proof:**
Let $G$ be a graph with $|G| = n$ and $\|G\| = m$. Suppose $m \ge 4n$.
Make a drawing of $G$ with $\text{cr}(G)$ crossings. Choose a subdrawing of an induced subgraph $H$ of $G$ by randomly choosing to include a vertex with probability $p$.

Let $|H| = n'$ and $\|H\| = m'$.
By observation: $\mathbb{E}(n') = np$

An edge of $G$ is in $H$ if and only if both of its endpoints are chosen. So, the probability of an edge being in $H$ is $p^2$.
So, $\mathbb{E}(m') = p^2m$.

Let $c'$ be the number of crossings in our drawing of $H$. Note that $c' \ge \text{cr}(H)$.
Every crossing in $G$ uses 4 distinct vertices, so the probability that it is in $H$ is $p^4$.
Therefore, $\mathbb{E}(c') = p^4 \cdot \text{cr}(G)$.

From our earlier corollary applied to $H$:
$$c' \ge \text{cr}(H) \ge m' - (3n' - 6) > m' - 3n'$$

Taking the expectation of both sides:
$$\mathbb{E}(c') > \mathbb{E}(m' - 3n') = \mathbb{E}(m') - 3\mathbb{E}(n')$$
$$p^4 \cdot \text{cr}(G) > p^2m - 3pn$$

*Aside: choose $p \in (0, 1]$ that gives a good bound.*
Set $p = \frac{4n}{m}$.
$$\left(\frac{4n}{m}\right)^4 \text{cr}(G) \ge \dots$$
$$\text{cr}(G) > \frac{m^3}{4^3 n^2} = \frac{m^3}{64n^2} \quad \square$$

## Comparison of Lower Bounds on $\text{cr}(G)$

| $G$ | $m - (3n - 6)$ | $\frac{m^3}{4^3 n^2}$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $K_n$ | $\sim \frac{n^2}{2}$ | **$\sim \frac{n^4}{8^3}$** |
| $K_{n/2, n/2}$ | $\sim \frac{n^2}{4}$ | **$\sim \frac{n^4}{4^6}$** |
| $m = 4n$ | **$\sim n + 6$** | $\sim n$ |
