## Chapter 6: Traversability 

### Section 6.1: Eulerian Circuits


**Key Definitions:**
* **Trail:** A walk where *no edge* is traversed more than once (though it may repeat vertices).
* **Circuit:** A closed trail of length 3 or more.
* **Eulerian Circuit (EC):** A circuit that uses *every edge* of a graph exactly once.
* **Eulerian Graph:** A graph containing an Eulerian circuit.
* **Eulerian Trail:** A trail containing *every edge* of a graph (but is not necessarily closed).

**True/False Concept Check:**
* **TRUE:** *Every circuit is a closed walk.* (By definition, it is closed and has no repeating edges, satisfying the conditions of a walk).
* **FALSE:** *Every circuit is a cycle.* (A circuit can repeat vertices, whereas a cycle cannot repeat vertices other than the start/end point).
* **FALSE:** *Every trail is a path.* (A trail can repeat vertices, but a path cannot).

**The Motivating Problem:**
The historical origin of this topic is the **Bridges of Königsberg** (now Kaliningrad, Russia) problem: *Can you go on a pleasant stroll and cross every bridge exactly once? (Maybe ending where you started, maybe not).*

## Proof: Eulerian Circuits and Even Degrees

This outlines a standard proof by contradiction showing that if every vertex in a connected graph has an even degree, the graph contains an Eulerian circuit.

**The Proof Steps:**
1.  **Setting up a maximal circuit:** Let $C$ be a maximal trail (the longest possible trail you can walk without repeating an edge) in graph $G$. Because every vertex in $G$ has an even degree, every time you enter a vertex, there must be an unused edge available to leave it—unless you have returned to your starting point. If you got stuck at any other vertex, it would mean you used an odd number of its edges (one to enter, plus any previous in/out pairs), which guarantees at least one unused edge remains. You could then add that edge to extend $C$, which contradicts the premise that $C$ is already maximal. Therefore, a maximal trail $C$ must end exactly where it started, making $C$ a closed circuit.
2.  **Suppose By Way Of Contradiction (BWOC):** Assume $E(C) \neq E(G)$. This means our maximal circuit $C$ does *not* contain all the edges of the graph $G$.
3.  **Create Subgraph $H$:** Let $H = G - E(C)$ (the graph formed by removing the edges of $C$ from $G$). 
    * *Note:* Because $C$ is a circuit, it uses exactly 2 edges every time it enters and leaves a vertex. If $G$ originally had all even degrees, subtracting the even number of edges used by $C$ means all vertices in $H$ must *also* have an even degree.
4.  **Find an intersection:** Because the original graph $G$ is connected, there must be at least one edge $xy \in E(H)$ such that the vertex $x$ is also part of our circuit $C$ ($x \in V(C)$).
5.  **Build a new trail $D$:** Let $D$ be the longest trail in $H$ starting at vertex $x$. Just like with $C$, because all vertices in $H$ have even degrees, $D$ must eventually get stuck at its starting point, making it a closed circuit as well.
6.  **The "Splice":** Splice the new circuit $D$ into the original circuit $C$ at vertex $x$. The path now looks like: $C \dots x \dots D \dots x \dots C$.
7.  **The Contradiction:** Because $xy \in E(D)$, the resulting spliced walk is a strictly longer closed circuit than $C$. Since the edges of $D$ are a subset of $H$ ($E(D) \subseteq E(H) = E(G) \setminus E(C)$), we have created a valid circuit larger than $C$. This contradicts our initial assumption that $C$ was the *maximum* possible circuit. 
8.  **Conclusion:** Therefore, the assumption that $E(C) \neq E(G)$ must be false. $E(C)$ must equal $E(G)$, proving the graph has a circuit that covers every single edge (an Eulerian circuit).
