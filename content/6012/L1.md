# Lecture 1: Introduction to Cyber-Physical System Security

**Course:** SE6012 - Cyber Physical System Security  
**Lecturer:** Dr. Shivam Bhasin  
**Academic Year:** 2026/27, Semester 1

> [!abstract] Lecture Overview
> This lecture introduces Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS), their characteristics, applications, architecture, and security challenges.
>
> The central idea is that **CPS security is different from traditional cybersecurity because cyberattacks can directly affect physical processes, potentially causing irreversible damage or harm to people.**
>
> Securing CPS requires integrating cybersecurity, physical-system control, safety engineering, and attack detection.

## 1. Introduction to Cyber-Physical Systems

### 1.1 What is a Cyber-Physical System?

A **Cyber-Physical System (CPS)** is a physical and engineered system whose operations are monitored, coordinated, controlled, and integrated by a computing and communication core.

A CPS combines two domains:

| Domain | Description |
|---|---|
| Physical | Natural or human-made systems governed by physical laws, generally operating in continuous time |
| Cyber | Computation, communication, and control using discrete, logical, and switched operations |

A CPS tightly integrates these two domains to achieve a particular purpose.

Examples include:
- Autonomous vehicles
- Smart manufacturing systems
- Smart electricity grids
- Medical devices
- Industrial robots
- Smart buildings

**Key idea:** A CPS is not simply a computer connected to a physical object. Its defining characteristic is the tight interaction between computation and physical processes.

### 1.2 The CPS Feedback Loop

The most important characteristic of a CPS is its **feedback loop**.

```text
       Physical Environment
                |
                v
             Sensors
                |
                v
       Cyber System / Controller
       (Computation and Decisions)
                |
                v
            Actuators
                |
                v
       Physical Environment
                |
                +----> Feedback to Sensors
```

The process consists of four steps:

1. Sensors collect information from the physical environment.
2. The cyber system processes the measurements.
3. The controller determines an appropriate action.
4. Actuators execute the action, changing the physical environment.

The sensors then measure the updated physical state, and the process repeats.

**Example: Autonomous vehicle**

An autonomous vehicle continuously:

1. Measures its speed and surroundings.
2. Processes the sensor information.
3. Determines whether to accelerate, brake, or steer.
4. Executes the control command.

The resulting movement is measured again.

> [!important] Why the Feedback Loop Matters for Security
> An attacker who manipulates sensor measurements can influence the controller's decisions.
>
> An incorrect digital decision can therefore cause an incorrect physical action.
>
> This is one of the fundamental reasons CPS security differs from conventional cybersecurity.

## 2. Why Are CPS Important?

### 2.1 Benefits of CPS

Integrating computation and communication with physical processes enables:

- **Safer systems:** Reducing human error and improving monitoring.
- **Greater efficiency:** Optimizing resources, energy, and performance.
- **Lower operating costs:** Automating expensive or repetitive processes.
- **New capabilities:** Enabling complex autonomous and intelligent systems.

For example, an autonomous vehicle combines sensing, computation, and physical control to support autonomous navigation.

A smart factory can adapt production based on demand without requiring extensive manual reconfiguration.

### 2.2 Technological and Economic Drivers

CPS development is driven by:

1. Decreasing costs of computation, networking, and sensing.
2. Increasing availability of powerful embedded computers.
3. Greater connectivity between devices.
4. Economic demand for automation and productivity.
5. More efficient use of infrastructure and resources.

Miniaturization allows increasingly sophisticated computing systems to be embedded in small physical objects.

### 2.3 CPS and Industry 4.0

| Industrial revolution | Main characteristics |
|---|---|
| Industry 1.0 | Mechanization, steam engines, mechanical power |
| Industry 2.0 | Mass production, assembly lines, electricity |
| Industry 3.0 | Automation, computers, electronics |
| Industry 4.0 | CPS, IoT, connected intelligent systems |

**CPS is a major enabling technology of Industry 4.0.**

## 3. Characteristics and Expectations of CPS

The lecture identifies several important characteristics of CPS.

### 3.1 Intelligence

Intelligent systems should be:

**Adaptive**
- Adjust to changing environments.
- Respond to changing inputs.
- Modify their behavior according to operating conditions.

Example: A smartphone reduces power consumption when idle and increases performance when required.

**Robust**
- Continue operating correctly despite disturbances.
- Maintain trustworthy behavior under hostile conditions.

### 3.2 Networking

CPS commonly involve multiple interconnected components.

Important networking characteristics include:

- **Communication:** Sharing information between devices.
- **Cooperation:** Coordinating actions between devices.
- **Cloud integration:** Supporting data storage and processing.

Example: A swarm of drones may communicate and dynamically coordinate its movement to avoid obstacles.

Cooperation is more than simply exchanging information. Devices must coordinate their actions to achieve a common objective.

### 3.3 Functionality

Important functional characteristics include:

- Productivity
- Flexibility
- Miniaturization

Flexibility allows systems to adapt to different operating requirements.

Miniaturization allows computing capabilities to be embedded into small or unconventional physical objects.

### 3.4 User-Friendliness

CPS should support:

- User interaction
- Human-machine communication
- Learning from user behavior
- Adaptation to user requirements

### 3.5 Expectations from CPS

| Property | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Autonomy | Ability to make decisions without continuous human intervention |
| Efficiency | Optimization of energy, memory, performance, or cost |
| Stability | Maintaining appropriate operating behavior |
| Reliability | Providing consistent operation over time |
| Accuracy | Producing correct measurements and outputs |
| Safety | Preventing unacceptable physical harm |
| Robustness | Tolerating disturbances and hostile conditions |
| Scalability | Supporting larger interconnected systems |
| Communication | Enabling data exchange and information sharing |

> [!important] Reliability vs. Robustness
> **Reliability** concerns consistent and correct operation over time.
>
> **Robustness** concerns maintaining appropriate operation despite disturbances or attacks.

## 5. CPS Architecture

A typical CPS has three layers:

1. Perception layer
2. Transmission layer
3. Application layer

```text
       +------------------------+
       |   Application Layer    |
       | Decision-Making        |
       | Data Analysis          |
       | User Interaction       |
       +------------------------+
                  ^
                  |
                  v
       +------------------------+
       |   Transmission Layer   |
       | Communication          |
       | Data Transfer          |
       +------------------------+
                  ^
                  |
                  v
       +------------------------+
       |    Perception Layer    |
       | Sensors and Actuators  |
       | Physical Environment   |
       +------------------------+
```

### 5.1 Perception Layer

Also called the **sensing layer**.

It provides the interface between the physical and digital worlds.

Components include:
- Sensors
- Actuators
- RFID tags and readers
- GPS
- Data aggregators

**Sensor:** Collects information from the physical environment.

**Actuator:** Executes physical actions based on control commands.

Examples:
- Temperature sensor
- Pressure sensor
- Motor
- Valve

### 5.2 Transmission Layer

Also called the **network or transport layer**.

Its purpose is to transfer information between the perception and application layers.

Communication technologies include:
- Wi-Fi
- Bluetooth
- ZigBee
- TCP/IP
- Cellular networks

The transmission layer carries sensor measurements to the controller and control commands back to physical devices.

### 5.3 Application Layer

Responsible for:
- Processing received information
- Running decision-making algorithms
- Generating control commands
- Data analysis
- User interaction

Examples:
- Smart traffic control
- Smart grid management
- Industrial monitoring systems

> [!tip] Remember the Three Layers
> **Perception = Sense and Act**
>
> **Transmission = Communicate**
>
> **Application = Process and Decide**

## 6. CPS vs. Internet of Things (IoT)

CPS and IoT are closely related and often overlap, but their primary focuses differ.

### 6.1 Cyber-Physical Systems

CPS focuses on the tight integration of:

- Physical processes
- Computation
- Communication
- Control

The defining feature is cyber-physical interaction.

### 6.2 Internet of Things

IoT focuses on the interconnection of devices that exchange information and coordinate activities.

IoT characteristics include:
- Interconnectivity
- Heterogeneity
- Dynamic changes
- Scalability

The network is not necessarily limited to the public internet.

### 6.3 Comparison

| CPS | IoT |
|---|---|
| Focuses on physical processes and computation | Focuses on interconnected devices |
| Emphasizes sensing, control, and feedback | Emphasizes communication and information exchange |
| Can operate without external network connectivity | Requires interconnected devices |
| Physical interaction is a defining feature | Physical interaction is not necessarily required |

**Example: Stabilizing spoon**

A spoon designed to compensate for hand tremors uses sensors and control mechanisms to stabilize its movement.

It is a CPS because it combines sensing, computation, and physical control.

It does not require internet connectivity.

**Example: Conventional wireless CCTV**

The lecturer presents a conventional wireless CCTV camera as an example of an IoT device that may not qualify as a CPS if it lacks active physical control.

Modern cameras with physical tracking mechanisms can also exhibit CPS characteristics.

### 6.4 Four-Layer IoT Architecture

The slides introduce a four-layer IoT architecture.

| Layer | Function |
|---|---|
| Perception | Physical devices, sensors, and actuators |
| Transport | Communication and data transmission |
| Processing | Data processing, storage, and analytics |
| Application | Decision-making and user interaction |

The processing layer may include:
- Edge computing
- Fog devices
- Cloud services
- Data storage

Compared with the three-layer CPS model, this IoT model explicitly separates the processing layer.

### 6.5 Heterogeneity and Security

IoT systems contain devices with different:

- Computational capabilities
- Communication protocols
- Hardware architectures
- Security capabilities

For example, a smart home may connect a smartphone, smartwatch, refrigerator, and smart lightbulb.

Not all devices provide the same security protections.

> [!warning] Weakest-Link Problem
> A weakly protected device can create a security vulnerability for a larger interconnected system.

# Part II: Cyber-Physical System Security

## 7. Why CPS Security Is Different

Traditional cybersecurity primarily protects digital information and computer systems.

CPS security must additionally consider the physical consequences of cyberattacks.

Slides 45–47 identify five distinctive characteristics.

### 7.1 Feedback Loops

Software directly influences physical behavior.

An attack can propagate in either direction:

```text
Cyber Attack -> Physical Consequence

Physical Manipulation -> Incorrect Cyber Decision
```

Example: Manipulating a vehicle's speed measurement can cause its controller to make an incorrect acceleration decision.

### 7.2 Real-Time Constraints

Many CPS must respond within strict deadlines.

A correct action performed too late may still be incorrect.

Examples:
- Delayed braking
- Delayed emergency shutdown
- Delayed pressure warning
- Delayed medical-device response

**Timing is part of correctness in CPS.**

### 7.3 Heterogeneity

CPS may integrate:
- Modern cloud services
- Old operating systems
- Industrial controllers
- Embedded devices
- Different communication protocols

Industrial programmable logic controllers (PLCs) may remain operational for decades.

Connecting these devices to modern networks creates additional attack surfaces and compatibility challenges.

### 7.4 Safety-Criticality

CPS failures may cause:
- Human injury
- Loss of life
- Infrastructure damage
- Environmental harm

### 7.5 Physical Consequences

Physical consequences may be irreversible.

Examples:
- A vehicle collision
- A chemical release
- Damage to industrial equipment
- Incorrect medical treatment

> [!important] Central Security Principle
> The physical consequences of CPS attacks distinguish them from many conventional cybersecurity incidents.
>
> Restoring software or data cannot necessarily reverse physical damage.

## 8. Why Conventional Cybersecurity Is Insufficient

Conventional cybersecurity methods include:

- Encryption
- Authentication
- Firewalls
- Data validation
- Intrusion detection

These methods protect information and communication.

However, they do not inherently address:
- Physical dynamics
- Real-time constraints
- Actuator behavior
- System stability
- Physical safety

### 8.1 Information Security vs. Secure Control

| Information security | Secure control |
|---|---|
| Computer science perspective | Control theory perspective |
| Focuses on data validation | Focuses on physical-system behavior |
| Protects confidentiality, integrity, and authentication | Protects stability, robustness, and resilience |
| Uses cryptography, firewalls, and intrusion detection | Uses robust control, fault-tolerant control, and fault detection |
| Does not inherently model physical dynamics | Does not inherently model intelligent adversaries |

A secure CPS requires both approaches.

### 8.2 Medical Device Example

An implantable medical device may communicate wirelessly with an external programmer.

An attacker who gains unauthorized programming access could modify its operating settings.

The lecture also highlights that wireless energy transfer is different from data communication.

Simply applying conventional data encryption does not automatically solve vulnerabilities associated with physical energy transfer.

Possible protections include:
- Secure authentication
- Challenge-response mechanisms
- Access control
- Device-specific physical security mechanisms

**Lesson:** CPS security mechanisms must account for the properties of the underlying physical process.

## 9. CPS Attack Surfaces

Each architectural layer presents different security threats.

### 9.1 Application Layer Attacks

Common threats:
- Malware
- Unauthorized commands
- Privilege escalation
- Data tampering
- Malicious code injection

These attacks target computation, control, or user-facing applications.

### 9.2 Transmission Layer Attacks

Common threats:
- Man-in-the-middle (MITM) attacks
- Replay attacks
- Spoofing
- Denial of service (DoS)
- Manipulation of transmitted information

**Man-in-the-middle attack:** An attacker intercepts communication and may modify the transmitted information.

**Replay attack:** Previously captured information or commands are transmitted again.

**Spoofing:** An attacker impersonates a legitimate source or provides false information.

**Denial of service:** An attacker disrupts the availability of communication or services.

### 9.3 Perception Layer Attacks

Common threats:
- Sensor manipulation
- GPS spoofing
- False-data injection
- Physical tampering
- Side-channel attacks
- Fault-injection attacks

These attacks target the physical devices and their interaction with the environment.

### 9.4 Attack Example: Manipulated Vehicle Speed

Suppose a vehicle is travelling at 80 km/h.

An attacker manipulates the reported speed so that the controller receives a measurement of 40 km/h.

The controller may conclude that the vehicle is moving too slowly and command further acceleration.

```text
Actual Speed: 80 km/h
        |
        v
Manipulated Measurement: 40 km/h
        |
        v
Controller Believes Vehicle Is Too Slow
        |
        v
Controller Commands Acceleration
        |
        v
Potentially Dangerous Physical Behavior
```

This illustrates how manipulating information can affect the physical environment.

The initial attack targets **integrity**, but its consequences may also affect safety.

### 9.5 Attack Summary

| CPS layer | Attack examples |
|---|---|
| Application | Malware, unauthorized commands, data tampering |
| Transmission | MITM, replay, spoofing, DoS |
| Perception | Sensor spoofing, false-data injection, physical tampering, side-channel and fault injection |

## 10. Types of CPS Attack Effects

Slide 55 identifies several possible consequences of CPS attacks.

| Attack effect | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Physical privacy violation | Revealing private information through physical measurements | Inferring occupancy from sensor data |
| Unauthorized actuation | Triggering a physical action without authorization | Unauthorized activation of an actuator |
| Incorrect actuation | Causing the wrong physical action | Manipulated speed data causing acceleration |
| Delayed actuation | Delaying an action beyond its required deadline | Delayed pressure warning |
| Prevented actuation | Blocking a required action | Preventing a robot from executing a command |

> [!important] Correct Commands Are Not Always Sufficient
> A CPS can become unsafe if an action is:
>
> - Incorrect
> - Unauthorized
> - Delayed
> - Prevented
>
> Both the content and timing of control actions matter.

## 11. Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Attack Classification

### 11.1 Common Vulnerabilities

The lecture identifies weaknesses including:

- Lack of secure communication
- Insecure default features
- Lack of authentication
- Lack of cryptography
- Lack of authorization
- Privacy and security weaknesses

These weaknesses may allow attackers to access, manipulate, or disrupt CPS components.

### 11.2 Attack Taxonomy

Attacks can be classified according to several dimensions.

| Dimension | Categories |
|---|---|
| Nature | External/internal, passive/active, malicious/rational |
| Target | Devices, servers, data |
| Scope | Small, moderate, large |
| Impact | Low, moderate, critical |
| Defensive capacity | Detection and correction, detection without correction, neither |

**Passive attack:** Primarily observes information without directly modifying system behavior.

**Active attack:** Manipulates data, commands, or system operation.

Attack classification helps organizations understand incidents, evaluate their consequences, and determine how effectively the system can detect and respond to them.

## 12. Safety vs. Security

Safety and security are related but distinct objectives.

### 12.1 Safety

Safety focuses on preventing accidental or unintended physical harm.

Examples:
- Robot emergency stop
- Protection against equipment failure
- Prevention of accidental industrial incidents

**Safety protects the physical world from the system.**

### 12.2 Security

Security focuses on protecting systems against deliberate and adaptive attacks.

Examples:
- Preventing unauthorized control
- Preventing malicious data manipulation
- Preventing deliberate disruption

**Security protects the system from malicious threats.**

### 12.3 Comparison

| Safety | Security |
|---|---|
| Accidental or unintended failures | Intentional attacks |
| Often models random, independent failures | Models intelligent, adaptive adversaries |
| Uses probabilistic failure rates | Emphasizes worst-case threats |
| Uses fault trees and FMEA | Uses threat modelling |
| Redundancy is an important protection | Attackers may deliberately defeat redundancy |
| Example standard: IEC 61508 | Requires adversarial security analysis |

Safety engineering often studies failure rates and the probability of accidental failures.

Security engineering must account for attackers who deliberately search for weaknesses and adapt their methods.

### 12.4 Interaction Between Safety and Security

Security breaches can create safety problems.

**Example: Water-treatment plant**

An attacker manipulates chemical dosing.

The resulting water may become unsafe for consumers.

Conversely, safety mechanisms can introduce security weaknesses.

**Example: Fire alarm**

A fire alarm may automatically unlock doors to allow evacuation.

This necessary safety response can temporarily weaken physical access control.

> [!important] Key Takeaway
> Safety and security must be designed together.
>
> Improving one without considering the other may introduce new vulnerabilities.

## 13. CPS Privacy

Privacy concerns the information that can be learned from data flowing through a system.

The lecture asks two important questions:

1. Can secret information flow to an adversary?
2. Can an adversary infer private information from publicly available measurements?

### Smart Meter Example

A smart electricity meter records household electricity consumption.

Patterns in consumption may reveal:
- Whether someone is at home
- When occupants leave or return
- Aspects of household activity

The data may be useful for optimizing electricity distribution, but it may also reveal private information.

This creates a trade-off between:

**Utility gained by sharing information** and **loss of privacy**.

Privacy can therefore be compromised through inference, even without directly accessing confidential records.

## 14. Real-World CPS Security Incidents

The lecture introduces four major examples of CPS attacks.

### 14.1 Maroochy Shire Sewage Incident (2000)

An attacker gained unauthorized access to wastewater infrastructure in Australia.

The attacker manipulated the system and caused sewage to be released into waterways.

**Security lesson:** Unauthorized access to industrial control systems can directly affect public infrastructure and the environment.

### 14.2 Stuxnet (2010)

Stuxnet was malware that targeted industrial control systems associated with Iranian uranium-enrichment centrifuges.

The attack manipulated equipment operation and caused physical damage.

**Security lesson:** Malware can manipulate industrial processes and damage physical equipment.

### 14.3 Ukraine Power-Grid Attack (2015)

An attack on Ukrainian electricity infrastructure disrupted power supplies to approximately 230,000 people.

The lecturer uses this case to illustrate the consequences of coordinated attacks against interconnected infrastructure.

**Security lesson:** Attacks on critical infrastructure can affect large populations and create cascading physical consequences.

### 14.4 Jeep Cherokee Security Demonstration (2015)

Security researchers demonstrated remote interference with a Jeep Cherokee while it was being driven.

The demonstration showed that digital vulnerabilities could affect vehicle functions and driver control.

**Security lesson:** Connected vehicles require protection against attacks that cross from digital systems into physical control.

### Summary

| Incident | System | Main consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Maroochy Shire | Wastewater infrastructure | Sewage release |
| Stuxnet | Industrial centrifuges | Physical equipment damage |
| Ukraine power grid | Electricity infrastructure | Power disruption |
| Jeep Cherokee | Connected vehicle | Remote interference with vehicle functions |

The common lesson is that a cyberattack can cause physical consequences beyond data loss or financial damage.

## 15. Defense in Depth

Defense in depth means using multiple complementary security mechanisms rather than relying on a single protection.

Possible mechanisms include:

- Authentication
- Encryption
- Access control
- Network protection
- Attack detection
- Physical-system monitoring
- Fault-tolerant control

A successful attack against one mechanism should not automatically compromise the entire system.

### Security as an Economic Problem

The lecturer explains that making a system completely impossible to attack is not a realistic design objective.

Instead, security measures should increase the attacker's required effort, resources, and cost.

The objective is to make exploitation increasingly difficult and less attractive.

### Long CPS Lifecycles

Industrial and transportation systems may remain operational for decades.

This creates several problems:

- Outdated operating systems
- Weak or obsolete cryptography
- Unpatched vulnerabilities
- Expensive upgrades
- Downtime risks
- Unsupported hardware
- Manufacturers that no longer exist
- Safety certification requirements that delay changes

A legacy industrial controller may need to communicate with a modern cloud service despite having very different security capabilities.

**Lesson:** CPS security must account for long-term maintenance and compatibility, not only the security of newly deployed devices.

## 16. Attack Detection and Monitoring

Attack detection aims to identify suspicious or malicious behavior.

The lecture discusses intrusion detection systems and monitors that examine system measurements and behavior.

### 16.1 Attack Detectability

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Detectable | A monitor raises an alarm indicating an attack |
| Identifiable | A monitor identifies the affected states or attacked components |
| Undetectable | The monitor does not identify the attack |

An attack can be detectable without being identifiable.

For example, a monitor may detect abnormal system behavior without knowing which sensor has been compromised.

### 16.2 Types of Monitors

**Static monitors**

Check measurement consistency without using system dynamics or measurements from different times.

**Dynamic monitors**

Use knowledge of system dynamics to evaluate whether observed behavior is consistent with expected operation.

**Active monitors**

Introduce auxiliary inputs to help reveal attacks.

### 16.3 Anomaly Detection vs. Intrusion Detection

An anomaly is a deviation from expected system behavior.

Possible causes include:
- Component degradation
- Environmental changes
- Unexpected interactions
- Unanticipated operating conditions
- Malicious attacks

An intrusion specifically involves malicious interference by an attacker.

| Anomaly detection | Intrusion detection |
|---|---|
| Identifies unexpected behavior | Identifies malicious interference |
| May detect faults or attacks | Focuses on attacks |
| Often uses expected system behavior | Often uses security-focused monitoring |

Not every anomaly is an intrusion.

The lecture notes that machine-learning methods are increasingly used for detection.

## 17. The CIA Triad

The CIA triad consists of three fundamental security goals:

1. Confidentiality
2. Integrity
3. Availability

It applies to digital systems and CPS.

### 17.1 Confidentiality

**Definition:** Protecting information from unauthorized access.

Only authorized entities should be able to read protected information.

Examples:
- Preventing unauthorized access to patient data
- Protecting cryptographic keys
- Preventing disclosure of sensitive sensor measurements

Possible protection:
- Encryption
- Access control

### 17.2 Integrity

**Definition:** Preventing unauthorized modification of information.

Data and commands should not be changed without authorization.

Examples:
- Preventing modification of sensor measurements
- Preventing unauthorized changes to control commands
- Protecting medical-device settings

Possible protection:
- Authentication
- Integrity checks
- Secure communication protocols

### 17.3 Availability

**Definition:** Ensuring information and services are accessible to authorized users when required.

In CPS, availability must also consider real-time deadlines.

Example:

If a control system must respond within 10 milliseconds, a security operation that takes three seconds may violate the system's timing requirements.

Possible protection:
- Resilient communication
- Fault tolerance
- Protection against denial-of-service attacks

### 17.4 CIA Summary

| Goal | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Confidentiality | Who can read the information? | Unauthorized access to medical data |
| Integrity | Who can modify the information? | Manipulated vehicle speed measurement |
| Availability | Is the information or service accessible when needed? | Delayed emergency control command |

> [!tip] CIA Memory Aid
> **Confidentiality = No unauthorized reading**
>
> **Integrity = No unauthorized modification**
>
> **Availability = Accessible when required**

### 17.5 Applying CIA to CPS

A single attack may affect more than one security objective.

For example:

**Manipulated sensor data**
- Directly compromises integrity.
- May cause incorrect physical control.
- May create a safety hazard.

**Delayed control command**
- Directly compromises availability.
- May prevent the system from meeting its real-time deadline.
- May create a safety hazard.

**Leaked smart-meter data**
- Compromises confidentiality or privacy.
- May reveal household occupancy patterns.

## Important Concepts to Understand

### Why is CPS security different from traditional cybersecurity?

Because CPS connect digital computation to physical processes.

Attacks can cross between cyber and physical components, must account for real-time constraints, and may cause irreversible physical harm.

### Why is conventional encryption insufficient for complete CPS security?

Encryption protects certain aspects of data communication but does not inherently protect physical dynamics, sensors, actuators, system stability, or real-time operation.

CPS requires cybersecurity and secure-control mechanisms to work together.

### Why is heterogeneity a security problem?

Different devices have different computational, communication, and security capabilities.

Older devices may contain outdated software or weak security mechanisms, creating vulnerabilities in the larger interconnected system.

### Why is safety not the same as security?

Safety primarily concerns accidental failures and unintended harm.

Security concerns deliberate attacks by intelligent adversaries.

An attacker may deliberately exploit weaknesses that conventional reliability analysis does not address.

### Why is availability particularly important in CPS?

Many CPS operate under strict real-time deadlines.

A control command that arrives too late may cause physical harm even if the command itself is correct.

---

> [!summary] Final Takeaway
> Cyber-Physical Systems tightly integrate computation, communication, and physical processes.
>
> This integration creates new capabilities but also introduces security challenges that conventional cybersecurity alone cannot solve.
>
> **Effective CPS security requires protecting information, communication, physical devices, control processes, and system safety together.**
>
> The most important difference is that attacks on CPS can cause real-world physical consequences that may be irreversible.
