# Part 1: Review of Previous Cryptography Concepts

## 1. Shannon Ciphers

A Shannon cipher consists of:

- Plaintext space $\mathcal{M}$
- Ciphertext space $\mathcal{C}$
- Key space $\mathcal{K}$
- Encryption algorithm $E$
- Decryption algorithm $D$

### Correctness

A cipher must allow the receiver to recover the original plaintext.

For every valid key $k$ and plaintext $m$:

$$
D_k(E_k(m))=m
$$

This means that encryption must be uniquely decryptable.

For a fixed key, different plaintexts cannot encrypt to the same ciphertext.

Otherwise, the receiver would not know which plaintext was originally encrypted.

## 2. Perfect Security

Shannon's definition of perfect security states that observing a ciphertext must not reveal any information about the plaintext.

Mathematically:

$$
\boxed{P(M=m\mid C=c)=P(M=m)}
$$

Here:

- $P(M=m)$ is the probability of plaintext $m$ before observing the ciphertext.
- $P(M=m\mid C=c)$ is the probability of plaintext $m$ after observing ciphertext $c$.

For perfect security, these probabilities must be equal for every plaintext and every ciphertext that can occur.

**The ciphertext must not change the attacker's knowledge about the plaintext.**

### Example

Suppose the three possible plaintexts are equally likely:

$$
P(M=0)=P(M=1)=P(M=2)=\frac13
$$

After observing any ciphertext, perfect security requires:

$$
P(M=0\mid C=c)=\frac13
$$

$$
P(M=1\mid C=c)=\frac13
$$

$$
P(M=2\mid C=c)=\frac13
$$

If even one of these probabilities changes, the cipher is not perfectly secure.

## 3. One-Time Pad

The one-time pad encrypts a message by XORing it with a uniformly random key.

Encryption:

$$
C=M\oplus K
$$

Decryption:

$$
M=C\oplus K
$$

This works because XOR is self-inverse:

$$
K\oplus K=0
$$

Therefore:

$$
(M\oplus K)\oplus K=M
$$

The one-time pad provides perfect secrecy when the key:

- Is uniformly random.
- Is at least as long as the message.
- Remains secret.
- Is never reused.

### Limitation

The key must be as long as the message.

If Alice wants to send a very long message, she must first securely share an equally long random key with Bob.

This is impractical for many applications.

Therefore, practical cryptography usually aims for computational security rather than perfect security.

## 4. Computational Security

Computational security assumes that an attacker has limited computational resources.

A cipher is computationally secure when an efficient adversary cannot extract useful information about the plaintext.

Unlike perfect security, computational security does not require security against attackers with unlimited time.

A sufficiently long secret key can make exhaustive key search computationally infeasible.

This motivates pseudorandom generators and stream ciphers, which use short secret keys to generate longer pseudorandom keystreams.

# Part 2: Exercise on Perfect Security

## 5. An Affine Cipher Modulo 3

The lecture considers the encryption function:

$$
\boxed{E_{(k_1,k_2)}(m)=(k_1m+k_2)\bmod 3}
$$

The plaintext space is:

$$
\mathcal{M}=\{0,1,2\}
$$

The lecturer examines three different key spaces.

### Case 1: Both Keys Are Nonzero

Let:

$$
k_1,k_2\in\mathbb{Z}_3^*=\{1,2\}
$$

There are four possible keys:

$$
(1,1),(1,2),(2,1),(2,2)
$$

The encryption table is:

| Plaintext | (1,1) | (1,2) | (2,1) | (2,2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | 2 | 1 | 2 |
| 1 | 2 | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| 2 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 0 |

For each fixed key, all three plaintexts produce different ciphertexts.

Therefore, the encryption function is uniquely decryptable.

**Conclusion: This is a correct cipher.**

However, it is not perfectly secure.

Consider ciphertext $C=0$.

From the table, plaintext $M=0$ can never produce ciphertext 0.

Thus:

$$
P(M=0\mid C=0)=0
$$

But before observing the ciphertext:

$$
P(M=0)=\frac13
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(M=0\mid C=0)\neq P(M=0)
$$

The ciphertext reveals that the plaintext cannot be zero.

**Conclusion: The cipher is correct but not perfectly secure.**

### Case 2: The Second Key Can Be Zero

Now let:

$$
k_1\in\{1,2\}
$$

$$
k_2\in\{0,1,2\}
$$

There are six possible keys.

The cipher remains correct because $k_1$ always has a multiplicative inverse modulo 3.

For uniformly selected keys, every plaintext produces each ciphertext under exactly two of the six keys.

Consequently, observing a ciphertext does not change the plaintext probabilities.

For uniformly distributed plaintexts:

$$
P(M=m\mid C=c)=\frac13
$$

for every plaintext $m$ and ciphertext $c$.

**Conclusion: This cipher is correct and perfectly secure when the keys are selected uniformly.**

### Case 3: Both Keys Can Be Zero

Now let:

$$
k_1,k_2\in\{0,1,2\}
$$

The value $k_1=0$ is possible.

For example:

$$
E_{(0,1)}(m)=1
$$

regardless of the plaintext.

Therefore:

$$
E_{(0,1)}(0)=E_{(0,1)}(1)=E_{(0,1)}(2)=1
$$

The receiver cannot determine which plaintext was encrypted.

**Conclusion: This is not a correct cipher and therefore cannot be a perfectly secure cipher.**

### Important Exam Principle

When evaluating a cipher:

1. Check whether encryption is uniquely decryptable for every key.
2. If it is a correct cipher, check perfect security.
3. Compare plaintext probabilities before and after observing a ciphertext.

A single counterexample is sufficient to disprove correctness or perfect security.

# Part 3: One-Time Pad Authentication Problem

## 6. Confidentiality Does Not Imply Authentication

The one-time pad provides perfect secrecy under its proper assumptions.

However, it does not provide message authentication.

An attacker can modify an encrypted message without knowing the key.

Suppose the original plaintext is:

```text
send $10 to Bob
```

The attacker wants the receiver to obtain:

```text
send $10 to Eve
```

The original ciphertext is:

$$
C=M\oplus K
$$

The attacker wants to construct:

$$
C'=M'\oplus K
$$

where $M'$ is the modified message.

Define the difference between the two messages:

$$
\Delta=M\oplus M'
$$

The attacker computes:

$$
\boxed{C'=C\oplus\Delta}
$$

### Why Does This Work?

Substitute the original ciphertext:

$$
C'=(M\oplus K)\oplus(M\oplus M')
$$

Rearrange the XOR operations:

$$
C'=M\oplus M\oplus K\oplus M'
$$

Since:

$$
M\oplus M=0
$$

we obtain:

$$
C'=K\oplus M'
$$

When the receiver decrypts:

$$
C'\oplus K=M'
$$

The receiver obtains the attacker's modified message.

### Main Lesson

Encryption and authentication are different security properties.

**Confidentiality** prevents unauthorized parties from learning a plaintext.

**Authentication** protects against unauthorized message modification.

The one-time pad provides confidentiality but does not automatically provide authentication.

Also, the same one-time-pad key must never be reused.

# Part 4: Pseudorandom Functions

## 7. PRG Versus PRF

The lecture reviews pseudorandom generators and introduces exercises involving pseudorandom functions.

### Pseudorandom Generator (PRG)

A PRG expands a short random seed into a longer pseudorandom bitstream.

$$
G:\{0,1\}^k\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
$$

where:

$$
\ell>k
$$

A PRG can be used to generate a keystream for a stream cipher.

### Pseudorandom Function (PRF)

A PRF is a keyed function:

$$
F_k:\{0,1\}^n\rightarrow\{0,1\}^m
$$

It takes a fixed-length input and produces a fixed-length output.

For each fixed key, it is a deterministic function.

This means:

$$
F_k(x)=F_k(x)
$$

Every time the same input is queried under the same key, the output is identical.

A secure PRF should be computationally indistinguishable from a uniformly random function.

A random function is also consistent: the same input always produces the same output.

---

## 8. The PRF Distinguishing Game

The lecturer models PRF security using a game between a challenger and an adversary.

### Step 1: Choose a World

The challenger chooses a random secret bit:

$$
b\leftarrow\{0,1\}
$$

If:

$$
b=1
$$

the adversary interacts with the real keyed function.

If:

$$
b=0
$$

the adversary interacts with an ideal random function.

### Step 2: The Adversary Makes Queries

The adversary chooses inputs:

$$
m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_q
$$

The challenger returns the corresponding outputs from the selected world.

The challenger uses the same world for every query in that game.

### Step 3: The Adversary Guesses

The adversary outputs:

$$
b'\in\{0,1\}
$$

Its goal is to guess the challenger's secret bit.

### Distinguishing Advantage

The advantage is defined as:

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Adv}(A)=
\left|
P[A=1\mid b=1]
-
P[A=1\mid b=0]
\right|
}
$$

The first probability is the probability that the adversary guesses "real" in the real world.

The second is the probability that the adversary guesses "real" in the random world.

The advantage measures how differently the adversary behaves in the two worlds.

An advantage of zero means that this particular strategy provides no distinguishing information.

An advantage of one means the strategy distinguishes the worlds perfectly.

A secure PRF should not permit an efficient adversary to obtain a significant distinguishing advantage.

# Part 5: PRF Security Exercises

## 9. Does Modifying a Secure PRF Preserve Security?

The lecturer assumes that $F_k$ is secure.

The question is whether different constructions of $G_k$ remain secure.

### Exercise A: Reverse the Output Bits

Define:

$$
G_k(m)=\operatorname{reverse}(F_k(m))
$$

Example:

$$
F_k(m)=1011
$$

Then:

$$
G_k(m)=1101
$$

Reversing the bits is a bijection.

The operation can be undone by reversing the bits again.

If an adversary could distinguish $G_k$ from random, it could reverse the output to distinguish $F_k$ from random.

Therefore, the transformation does not introduce a distinguishing pattern.

**Conclusion: The construction remains secure.**

### Exercise B: XOR the Output with the Input

Define:

$$
G_k(m)=F_k(m)\oplus m
$$

The adversary knows $m$ because it chooses the input.

It can recover the original function output:

$$
F_k(m)=G_k(m)\oplus m
$$

XORing a uniformly random string with a known fixed string preserves its uniform distribution.

Therefore, an efficient distinguisher for $G_k$ would also provide a distinguisher for $F_k$.

**Conclusion: The construction remains secure.**

### Exercise C: XOR Outputs for Related Inputs

Define:

$$
G_k(m)=F_k(m)\oplus F_k(m\oplus0001)
$$

The second input is obtained by flipping the final bit of the first input.

Consider two queries:

$$
m_1=0000
$$

$$
m_2=0001
$$

The first output is:

$$
G_k(0000)=F_k(0000)\oplus F_k(0001)
$$

The second output is:

$$
G_k(0001)=F_k(0001)\oplus F_k(0000)
$$

Because XOR is commutative:

$$
\boxed{G_k(0000)=G_k(0001)}
$$

The two outputs are always identical.

### Distinguishing Strategy

The adversary:

1. Queries `0000`.
2. Queries `0001`.
3. Compares the two outputs.
4. Guesses "real" if they are identical.
5. Guesses "random" otherwise.

In the real world:

$$
P[A=1\mid b=1]=1
$$

For an ideal random function with 4-bit outputs, the outputs for two distinct inputs match with probability:

$$
P[A=1\mid b=0]=\frac1{16}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\operatorname{Adv}(A)=1-\frac1{16}
$$

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Adv}(A)=\frac{15}{16}}
$$

**Conclusion: The construction is insecure.**

A single output may look random, but the relationship between two outputs reveals the construction.

### Exercise D: Replace the Last Output Bit with Zero

Suppose $G_k$ replaces the last bit of $F_k(m)$ with zero.

Example:

$$
F_k(m)=1011
$$

but:

$$
G_k(m)=1010
$$

The last bit of every output is zero.

The adversary makes one query and checks the final bit.

Its strategy is:

- If the final bit is zero, guess "real".
- If the final bit is one, guess "random".

In the real world, the final bit is always zero:

$$
P[A=1\mid b=1]=1
$$

In the ideal random world, the final bit is zero with probability one-half:

$$
P[A=1\mid b=0]=\frac12
$$

The advantage is:

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Adv}(A)=1-\frac12=\frac12
}
$$

**Conclusion: The construction is insecure.**

### Summary of PRF Exercises

| Construction | Secure? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Reverse output bits | Yes | Reversible permutation |
| XOR output with input | Yes | Known reversible transformation |
| XOR related-input outputs | No | Related queries always produce identical outputs |
| Replace final bit with zero | No | Predictable output pattern |

The main lesson is:

> A secure cryptographic primitive does not automatically produce a secure construction when it is modified or combined with other operations.

# Part 6: Definition of Block Ciphers

## 10. What Is a Block Cipher?

A block cipher encrypts fixed-length plaintext blocks into ciphertext blocks of the same length.

A block cipher is defined as:

$$
\boxed{
E:\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
}
$$

where:

$$
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{C}=\{0,1\}^n
$$

For every fixed key $K$, the encryption function:

$$
E_K:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
$$

must be a permutation.

This means that encryption is a bijection.

Every plaintext corresponds to exactly one ciphertext, and every ciphertext corresponds to exactly one plaintext.

### Why Must a Block Cipher Be a Permutation?

Suppose two different plaintexts produce the same ciphertext:

$$
E_K(M_1)=E_K(M_2)
$$

where:

$$
M_1\neq M_2
$$

The receiver would not know which plaintext to recover.

This violates correctness.

Therefore, distinct plaintexts must produce distinct ciphertexts under a fixed key.

### Typical Block and Key Lengths

The slides identify common block lengths such as:

- 64 bits
- 128 bits

Common key lengths include:

- 128 bits
- 192 bits
- 256 bits

The block length and key length are different parameters.

For example, a cipher can use a 128-bit block and a 256-bit key.

### Toy Block Cipher

The slides demonstrate a 3-bit block cipher.

Let:

$$
M=(M_1,M_2,M_3)
$$

and:

$$
K=(K_1,K_2,K_3)
$$

Encryption XORs the message with the key and swaps the first two bits:

$$
C_1=M_2\oplus K_2
$$

$$
C_2=M_1\oplus K_1
$$

$$
C_3=M_3\oplus K_3
$$

This transformation is reversible, so it defines a correct block cipher.

However, correctness alone does not guarantee cryptographic security.

---

## 11. Stream Ciphers Versus Block Ciphers

| Property | Stream cipher | Block cipher |
|---|---|---|
| Main output | Keystream | Fixed-length ciphertext block |
| Ideal object | Random-looking bits | Random permutation |
| Typical operation | XOR keystream with plaintext | Apply a keyed permutation |
| Block length | Generates as much keystream as needed | Processes fixed-length blocks |
| Invertibility requirement | Keystream generator need not be invertible | Encryption must be invertible |

The lecturer uses ChaCha as an example of a stream cipher.

ChaCha uses a permutation over a 512-bit internal state containing key material, an initial state, and a counter.

The counter allows the construction to generate successive keystream blocks.

A block cipher instead directly maps one fixed-length block to another under a fixed key.

Block ciphers can also be used to encrypt longer messages through appropriate encryption schemes, which are studied later in the course.

# Part 7: Ideal Block Ciphers and PRP Security

## 12. Why Is a Random Permutation the Ideal?

A random function can map two different inputs to the same output.

For a block cipher, this would violate unique decryptability.

Therefore, the ideal block cipher is not an arbitrary random function.

It is a uniformly random permutation.

Let:

$$
\operatorname{Perm}(n)
$$

denote the set of all permutations over $n$-bit strings.

An ideal permutation is selected uniformly:

$$
\pi\xleftarrow{\$}\operatorname{Perm}(n)
$$

A secure block cipher under a random fixed key should be computationally indistinguishable from this ideal permutation.

### Number of Possible Permutations

An $n$-bit block has:

$$
2^n
$$

possible inputs.

The number of permutations over these inputs is:

$$
\boxed{(2^n)!}
$$

For example, with 3-bit blocks:

$$
2^3=8
$$

There are:

$$
8!=40320
$$

possible permutations.

For 64-bit blocks, the number of possible permutations is:

$$
(2^{64})!
$$

Each fixed key of a block cipher selects a permutation from this space.

---

## 13. Pseudorandom Permutation (PRP) Security

The PRP security game compares a real block cipher with an ideal random permutation.

### Real World

The challenger chooses a random secret key:

$$
K\xleftarrow{\$}\mathcal{K}
$$

The adversary can query:

$$
E_K(M)
$$

### Ideal World

The challenger chooses a uniformly random permutation:

$$
\pi\xleftarrow{\$}\operatorname{Perm}(n)
$$

The adversary can query:

$$
\pi(M)
$$

The adversary makes encryption queries and tries to determine which world it is interacting with.

The PRP advantage is:

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Adv}^{\mathrm{PRP}}_E(A)
=
\left|
P[A=1\mid\text{real}]
-
P[A=1\mid\text{ideal}]
\right|
}
$$

A secure PRP should not permit an efficient adversary to obtain a significant advantage.

### Important Detail

The same plaintext always produces the same ciphertext under a fixed key.

This also holds for the ideal random permutation.

Therefore, observing that repeated plaintexts produce repeated ciphertexts does not distinguish a block cipher from a random permutation.

---

## 14. Strong Pseudorandom Permutation (SPRP) Security

Strong PRP security gives the adversary more capabilities.

In addition to encryption queries, the adversary can make decryption queries.

In the real world, it can access:

$$
E_K
$$

and:

$$
E_K^{-1}
$$

In the ideal world, it can access:

$$
\pi
$$

and:

$$
\pi^{-1}
$$

The adversary tries to distinguish the real encryption/decryption pair from the ideal permutation and its inverse.

### PRP Versus SPRP

| Security notion | Encryption queries | Decryption queries |
|---|---|---|
| PRP | Yes | No |
| SPRP | Yes | Yes |

SPRP is the stronger security requirement.

A construction may be secure when the adversary can only encrypt but insecure when decryption queries are also available.

This distinction becomes important when analyzing Feistel networks.

# Part 8: Block Cipher Design Goals

## 15. Confusion and Diffusion

Shannon identified two important design principles for secure ciphers:

1. Diffusion
2. Confusion

## 16. Diffusion

Diffusion means that a small change in the input should spread across many output bits.

For example, changing a single plaintext bit should potentially affect many ciphertext bits.

The goal is to prevent a simple relationship between individual plaintext bits and ciphertext bits.

### Avalanche Criterion

The slides introduce the strict avalanche criterion.

When one input bit is flipped, each output bit should change with probability approximately:

$$
\boxed{\frac12}
$$

This is a statistical property measured across inputs.

It does not mean that exactly half of the ciphertext bits must change every time.

### Example

Original plaintext:

```text
10110010
```

Modified plaintext:

```text
10110011
```

Only the last plaintext bit changes.

A cipher with good diffusion should spread this change across many ciphertext positions.

Diffusion can be implemented efficiently using linear transformations and bit permutations.

However, diffusion alone is not sufficient for security.

---

## 17. Confusion

Confusion means that the secret key affects the ciphertext in a complex and nonlinear way.

Ideally, each ciphertext bit should depend on many or all key bits.

An attacker who does not know the key should not be able to predict ciphertexts or efficiently recover the key using simple algebraic relationships.

Nonlinear operations are especially important for confusion.

### Difference Between Confusion and Diffusion

| Concept | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Diffusion | Spread the influence of input changes across the output |
| Confusion | Make the relationship between the key and ciphertext complex and nonlinear |

A secure block cipher needs both.

---

# Part 9: Linear and Nonlinear Functions

## 18. Linear Functions

The slides define a linear function over a field as:

$$
F(x)=Lx
$$

where $L$ is a matrix.

A linear function satisfies:

$$
\boxed{F(x+\Delta)=F(x)+F(\Delta)}
$$

Over binary vectors, addition is XOR:

$$
F(x\oplus\Delta)=F(x)\oplus F(\Delta)
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
F(x)\oplus F(x\oplus\Delta)=F(\Delta)
}
$$

This means the output difference depends only on the input difference.

It does not depend on the original input.

### Why Is This Predictable?

Suppose an attacker observes:

$$
F(x)=y
$$

and:

$$
F(x\oplus\Delta)=\hat y
$$

The attacker learns the output difference:

$$
\delta=y\oplus\hat y
$$

For a new input $x'$, the attacker can predict:

$$
F(x'\oplus\Delta)=F(x')\oplus\delta
$$

Thus, knowing three input-output relationships can allow the attacker to derive a fourth.

This is the problem with relying only on linear transformations.

Even if a linear operation spreads differences across many output bits, those differences remain predictable.

---

## 19. Affine Functions

The slides also define an affine function:

$$
F(x)=Lx+c
$$

where $c$ is a constant.

An affine function satisfies:

$$
F(x+\Delta)=F(x)+F(\Delta)-F(0)
$$

Over binary vectors, subtraction and addition are both XOR.

For differences, the constant term cancels.

Therefore, affine functions also have predictable difference relationships.

---

## 20. Nonlinear Functions

Nonlinear functions break the simple difference relationships found in linear functions.

The lecture introduces Boolean AND as an example of a nonlinear operation.

For individual bits:

$$
x_1\land x_2=x_1x_2
$$

An example nonlinear Boolean function is:

$$
y=x_1x_2\oplus x_3
$$

The product term $x_1x_2$ introduces nonlinearity.

The lecturer explains that Boolean functions can be represented using combinations of XOR and bit products, known as algebraic normal form.

Real block ciphers use more complicated nonlinear relationships involving plaintext and key bits.

The goal is to prevent attackers from efficiently solving the resulting equations to recover secret information.

**Important:** Nonlinearity is a necessary design ingredient, but a nonlinear function is not automatically a secure cipher.

---

# Part 10: Round-Iterated Ciphers

## 21. Why Use Multiple Rounds?

Instead of implementing one enormous, complicated encryption function, block ciphers usually repeat a relatively simple round function.

A key schedule derives round keys:

$$
K_1,K_2,\ldots,K_r
$$

The cipher then applies a sequence of rounds:

$$
M\xrightarrow{R_{K_1}}S_1
\xrightarrow{R_{K_2}}S_2
\rightarrow\cdots
\xrightarrow{R_{K_r}}C
$$

Each round contributes confusion and diffusion.

Initially, a small input change may influence only a few state bits.

After additional rounds, the influence spreads to more parts of the state.

After sufficiently many appropriately designed rounds, the ciphertext can depend on many plaintext and key bits through complex nonlinear relationships.

The lecture introduces three major construction principles:

1. Substitution–Permutation Networks (SPNs)
2. Feistel Networks
3. Addition–Rotation–XOR (ARX) Constructions

---

# Part 11: Substitution–Permutation Networks

## 22. SPN Structure

An SPN combines three operations:

1. Round-key addition
2. Nonlinear substitution
3. Linear diffusion or permutation

A simplified round is:

```text
Input state
    |
    v
XOR with round key
    |
    v
Substitution layer (S-boxes)
    |
    v
Linear/permutation layer
    |
    v
Next round
```

Each component has a specific purpose.

### Round-Key Addition

The state is combined with a round key, commonly using XOR:

$$
Y=X\oplus K_i
$$

This introduces dependence on the secret key.

### Substitution Layer

The substitution layer applies nonlinear functions called S-boxes.

These provide confusion.

### Linear Layer

The linear layer spreads information across the state.

It provides diffusion.

The output of one S-box may be distributed into several S-boxes in the next round.

Repeating the round allows changes to spread further through the state.

---

## 23. What Is an S-Box?

An S-box, or substitution box, is a nonlinear function often implemented as a lookup table.

For example:

$$
S:\{0,1\}^4\rightarrow\{0,1\}^4
$$

A 4-bit S-box has 16 possible inputs.

Its output is determined by a fixed table.

For example, the lecture's SPN exercise uses the following entries:

| Input | Output |
|---|---|
| 0000 | 1100 |
| 0001 | 0101 |
| 0010 | 0110 |
| 0011 | 1011 |
| 0100 | 1001 |
| 0101 | 0000 |
| 0110 | 1010 |
| 0111 | 1101 |
| 1000 | 0011 |
| 1001 | 1110 |
| 1010 | 1111 |
| 1011 | 1000 |
| 1100 | 0100 |
| 1101 | 0111 |
| 1110 | 0001 |
| 1111 | 0010 |

For example:

$$
S(1001)=1110
$$

The S-box is a deterministic function, not a new random table generated for every encryption.

---

## 24. SPN Calculation Example

The slides provide:

$$
X_0=0010
$$

$$
X_1=1011
$$

with round-key parts:

$$
K_0^0=1011
$$

$$
K_1^0=0110
$$

### Step 1: XOR the Inputs with the Round Keys

For the first part:

$$
Y_0=0010\oplus1011
$$

$$
\boxed{Y_0=1001}
$$

For the second part:

$$
Y_1=1011\oplus0110
$$

$$
\boxed{Y_1=1101}
$$

### Step 2: Apply the S-Box

Look up the two values in the substitution table:

$$
Z_0=S(1001)=1110
$$

$$
Z_1=S(1101)=0111
$$

The state after substitution is:

$$
Z=11100111
$$

### Step 3: Apply the Linear Layer

The linear layer permutes the bits according to the wiring specified in the slide diagram.

The purpose is to distribute output bits from the S-boxes into different positions.

This allows them to influence different S-boxes in later rounds.

as examples of SPN-based block ciphers.

# Part 12: Feistel Networks

## 25. Feistel Network Structure

A Feistel network splits a plaintext block into two halves:

$$
M=L_0\parallel R_0
$$

The lecture slides use the following round equations:

$$
\boxed{
L_i=R_{i-1}\oplus F_i(K_i,L_{i-1})
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
R_i=L_{i-1}
}
$$

Here:

- $L_{i-1}$ is the left half before the round.
- $R_{i-1}$ is the right half before the round.
- $K_i$ is the round key.
- $F_i$ is the round function.
- $L_i,R_i$ are the output halves.

The round function takes the left half and a round key.

Its output is XORed with the right half.

The original left half becomes the new right half.

A simplified round is:

```text
       L(i-1)              R(i-1)
          |                   |
          v                   |
       F(Ki, L)               |
          |                   |
          +-------- XOR ------+
                       |
                       v
                      Li

       L(i-1) ----------------> Ri
```

The two halves change roles between rounds.

---

## 26. Why Does the Round Function Not Need to Be Invertible?

A major advantage of Feistel networks is that the round function $F_i$ does not need to be a permutation.

The complete Feistel round remains invertible.

Given:

$$
L_i=R_{i-1}\oplus F_i(K_i,L_{i-1})
$$

and:

$$
R_i=L_{i-1}
$$

we can recover the previous left half:

$$
L_{i-1}=R_i
$$

Then recover the previous right half:

$$
R_{i-1}=L_i\oplus F_i(K_i,R_i)
$$

Therefore, the complete input can be recovered without computing an inverse of $F_i$.

**This is why Feistel networks can construct invertible block ciphers from round functions that are not themselves invertible.**

### Examples

The slides identify:

- DES
- CLEFIA

as examples of Feistel-based block ciphers.

DES is introduced only at a high level in this lecture.

L_0=0010

$$
R_0=1011

The round functions are given by lookup tables.

From the first table:

$$
F_1(0010)=0001

### Round 1

Calculate the new left half:

$$
L_1=1011\oplus0001
$$

$$
\boxed{L_1=1010}
$$

The new right half is:

$$

$$

$$

### Round 2

From the second lookup table:

$$

The new right half is:

Final state:

# Part 13: ARX Constructions

## 28. Addition–Rotation–XOR

ARX constructions combine three operations:

- Addition
- Rotation
- XOR

The operations are repeated to construct complicated nonlinear transformations.

### Modular Addition

For $n$-bit words, addition is performed modulo:

$$
2^n
$$

For 4-bit words:

$$
1011+0100=1111
$$

If the sum exceeds the maximum 4-bit value, the overflow is discarded.

For example:

$$
1111+0010=10001
$$

Modulo $2^4=16$:

$$
\boxed{0001}
$$

### Bitwise Rotation

Rotation moves bits circularly.

For example, rotate `1011` left by one position:

$$
\operatorname{ROTL}_1(1011)=0111
$$

The leftmost bit wraps around to the rightmost position.

Unlike a logical shift, rotation does not discard bits.

### XOR

XOR combines bit strings:

$$
1011\oplus0110=1101
$$

### Why Combine These Operations?

Modular addition and XOR interact nonlinearly.

Rotation causes different bit positions to interact across rounds.

Together, these operations help create confusion and diffusion.

They are efficient because ordinary processors provide direct support for addition, rotation, and XOR.

### Examples

The slides mention:

- Speck
- Salsa/ChaCha permutations

The lecture also uses a small Speck-style ARX round to demonstrate rotation, modular addition, and XOR with a round key.

The important idea is that complex transformations can be built by repeatedly applying very simple operations.

# Part 14: Security of Feistel Networks

## 29. How Many Feistel Rounds Are Needed?

The slides analyze the security of Feistel networks using independent random round functions.

The theoretical results are:

| Number of rounds | PRP security | SPRP security |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Insecure | Insecure |
| 2 | Insecure | Insecure |
| 3 | Secure under the stated assumptions | Insecure |
| 4 or more | Secure under the stated assumptions | Secure under the stated assumptions |

These theoretical results assume appropriately secure, independent round functions and sufficiently limited numbers of queries.

They do not mean that any arbitrary four-round Feistel construction is secure.

Real ciphers often use simple round functions and require many more rounds.

---

## 30. Why Is One Feistel Round Insecure?

Recall the Feistel round equations:

$$
L_1=R_0\oplus F_1(K_1,L_0)
$$

$$
R_1=L_0
$$

The output right half is exactly equal to the input left half:

$$
\boxed{R_1=L_0}
$$

An adversary can exploit this relationship.

### Attack

1. Choose a plaintext $(L_0,R_0)$.
2. Obtain its ciphertext $(L_1,R_1)$.
3. Check whether $R_1=L_0$.

In the real one-round Feistel construction, this equality always holds.

For an ideal random permutation, it does not always hold.

Therefore, a single encryption query can distinguish the construction from a random permutation with significant advantage.

**Conclusion: One Feistel round is insecure.**

---

## 31. Why Are Two Feistel Rounds Insecure?

This is the main Feistel security exercise in the lecture.

The slides show that after two rounds:

$$
\boxed{
L_2=R_0\oplus F_1(L_0)
}
$$

The round key is omitted from the notation for simplicity.

The adversary chooses two plaintexts with the same left half but different right halves.

First plaintext:

$$
M_1=(L_0,R_0)
$$

Second plaintext:

$$
M_2=(L_0,R_0\oplus\Delta)
$$

where:

$$
\Delta\neq0
$$

The left half is unchanged.

Therefore, the input to $F_1$ is unchanged.

Because the round function is deterministic:

$$
F_1(L_0)=F_1(L_0)
$$

### First Encryption

The left half after two rounds is:

$$
L_2=R_0\oplus F_1(L_0)
$$

### Second Encryption

The modified input has:

$$
R_0'=R_0\oplus\Delta
$$

Therefore:

$$
L_2'=R_0'\oplus F_1(L_0)
$$

Substitute $R_0'$:

$$
L_2'=(R_0\oplus\Delta)\oplus F_1(L_0)
$$

### Compare the Two Outputs

Calculate:

$$
L_2\oplus L_2'
$$

Substitute both expressions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_2\oplus L_2'
&=
R_0\oplus F_1(L_0)\\
&\quad\oplus R_0\oplus\Delta\oplus F_1(L_0)
\end{aligned}
$$

The repeated terms cancel:

$$
R_0\oplus R_0=0
$$

$$
F_1(L_0)\oplus F_1(L_0)=0
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{L_2\oplus L_2'=\Delta}
$$

### What Does This Mean?

The adversary changes the right half of the plaintext by $\Delta$.

The same difference appears in the left half of the output after two rounds.

The adversary can predict this output relationship without knowing either round function or the secret key.

This relationship always holds in the real two-round Feistel construction.

It is not guaranteed for an ideal random permutation.

Therefore, an adversary can distinguish the two-round Feistel construction from a random permutation.

**Conclusion: Two-round Feistel networks are not PRP-secure.**

### Why Is This Attack Important?

The attack demonstrates that individual ciphertexts may look random while relationships between multiple ciphertexts reveal the cipher's structure.

This is the same idea encountered earlier in the PRF exercise involving:

$$
G_k(m)=F_k(m)\oplus F_k(m\oplus0001)
$$

In both cases, the adversary uses carefully chosen related inputs to identify a predictable relationship.

---

## 32. Three-Round Feistel Networks

The slides state that a three-round Feistel network can provide PRP security when the round functions are independent and sufficiently secure.

This means an efficient adversary with encryption-only access cannot distinguish the construction from a random permutation with significant advantage, within the stated query bounds.

However, three rounds do not provide SPRP security.

An adversary with both encryption and decryption access can still construct a distinguishing attack.

The lecturer does not require the formal proof of this result.

---

## 33. Four-Round Feistel Networks

Four Feistel rounds can provide SPRP security under the stated assumptions.

This means that even an adversary with encryption and decryption access cannot efficiently distinguish the construction from an ideal random permutation and its inverse, within the relevant query bounds.

The slides give the general form:

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Adv}(q)=O\left(\frac{q^2}{2^n}\right)
}
$$

where:

- $q$ is the number of queries.
- $n$ is the bit length of each Feistel half.
- $O(\cdot)$ describes an asymptotic upper bound.

When the number of queries is sufficiently small relative to $2^{n/2}$, the bound is small.

The lecture introduces this result conceptually rather than deriving the proof.

### Main Lesson

The security of a cryptographic construction depends on:

1. The security of its underlying components.
2. The way those components are combined.
3. The number of rounds.
4. The capabilities and resources of the adversary.

A secure round function does not automatically make a one-round or two-round Feistel network secure.

# Final Summary

## Key Definitions

| Concept | Definition |
|---|---|
| Correct cipher | Encryption can always be uniquely decrypted using the correct key |
| Perfect security | Observing a ciphertext does not change plaintext probabilities |
| PRG | Expands a short seed into a longer pseudorandom bitstream |
| PRF | Keyed function computationally indistinguishable from a random function |
| Block cipher | Family of keyed permutations over fixed-length blocks |
| PRP | Block cipher indistinguishable from a random permutation under encryption queries |
| SPRP | Block cipher indistinguishable from a random permutation even with encryption and decryption queries |
| Diffusion | Spreads small input changes across many output bits |
| Confusion | Creates complex nonlinear relationships involving the secret key |
| SPN | Combines key addition, substitution, and diffusion layers |
| Feistel network | Constructs an invertible cipher by mixing and exchanging two state halves |
| ARX | Constructs complex transformations using addition, rotation, and XOR |

## Important Formulas

### Correctness

$$
\boxed{D_k(E_k(m))=m}
$$

### Perfect Security

$$
\boxed{P(M=m\mid C=c)=P(M=m)}
$$

### One-Time Pad Encryption

$$
\boxed{C=M\oplus K}
$$

### OTP Ciphertext Modification

$$
\boxed{C'=C\oplus M\oplus M'}
$$

### Distinguishing Advantage

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Adv}(A)=
\left|
P[A=1\mid\text{real}]
-
P[A=1\mid\text{ideal}]
\right|
}
$$

### Number of Permutations on $n$-Bit Blocks

$$
\boxed{(2^n)!}
$$

### Linear Function Property

$$
\boxed{
F(x\oplus\Delta)=F(x)\oplus F(\Delta)
}
$$

### Feistel Round

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
L_i&=R_{i-1}\oplus F_i(K_i,L_{i-1})\\
R_i&=L_{i-1}
\end{aligned}
}
$$

### Two-Round Feistel Distinguishing Relationship

$$
\boxed{
L_2\oplus L_2'=\Delta
}
$$

### Theoretical Feistel Security

| Rounds | Security |
|---|---|
| 1 | Insecure |
| 2 | Insecure |
| 3 | PRP-secure under the stated assumptions |
| 4 or more | SPRP-secure under the stated assumptions |

# What to Focus on for the Exam

The lecturer emphasizes understanding the concepts and being able to apply them to small examples.

The following are particularly important:

1. **Correctness and perfect security:** Determine whether a proposed cipher is uniquely decryptable and whether observing a ciphertext changes plaintext probabilities.

2. **OTP modification:** Understand how XOR allows an attacker to modify an encrypted message without knowing the key.

3. **PRF security games:** Construct simple distinguishers, identify output patterns, and calculate distinguishing advantages.

4. **Block cipher definition:** Explain why a block cipher must be a permutation for every fixed key.

5. **PRP versus SPRP:** Understand the difference between encryption-only access and encryption-plus-decryption access.

6. **Confusion and diffusion:** Explain their purposes and why nonlinear operations are necessary.

7. **SPN calculations:** Perform round-key XOR, S-box lookups, and bit permutations.

8. **Feistel calculations:** Compute successive round outputs using the round equations.

9. **ARX operations:** Understand modular addition, bit rotations, and XOR.

10. **Two-round Feistel attack:** Understand how two related plaintext queries produce a predictable ciphertext difference.

11. **Three- and four-round Feistel security:** Know the qualitative PRP and SPRP results and the assumptions under which they hold.

The lecturer explicitly states that students are not expected to memorize complicated algebraic formulas or reproduce the formal Feistel security proofs.

The main objective is to understand how secure block ciphers are constructed and how an adversary can exploit predictable patterns in incorrectly designed constructions.

---

## Core Takeaway

A block cipher is a keyed permutation that should behave like a uniformly random permutation to an efficient adversary.

Secure block cipher design relies on combining confusion, diffusion, and repeated rounds.

However, even when the underlying components are secure, the complete construction may still be insecure if its structure creates predictable relationships between inputs and outputs.

**The central lesson of Lecture 4 is that cryptographic security depends not only on the individual components but also on how those components are combined.**
